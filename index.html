Abandoned but Not Forgotten

Five machines NASA left behind: funny, true, and a little bit emotional.

NASA's own list of things left on the Moon has more than 800 items: two golf balls, a pair of nail clippers and 96 bags of astronaut waste. Yes, that kind. But this story is about the other things we left behind: machines that worked overtime and stayed on the Moon, on Mars or in deep space, because bringing them home was impossible. Almost every one of them was supposed to quit early. They didn't. Their message: "Who told you that you can't?"

  • Moon Buggy: 3 driven on the Moon in 1971 and 1972. All 3 are still parked there.
  • Spirit: planned for 90 Mars days. Worked about 20 times longer.
  • Opportunity: planned for 90 Mars days and about 1 km. Worked 5,111 Mars days and drove 45.16 km.
  • Ingenuity: planned for 5 flights. Flew 72 times.
  • Voyager 1: launched in 1977. Still sending from about 25.9 billion km away.

1. The Moon Buggy: fastest car on the Moon (only car on the Moon)

Apollo 11's astronauts covered less than 1 km on foot in total. Then NASA built a battery-powered Moon car that folded up inside the lander. The Lunar Roving Vehicle drove on Apollo 15, 16 and 17. Top speed: about 8 mph (13 km/h). On a downhill run, Gene Cernan hit 11.2 mph (18 km/h), the unofficial lunar land speed record.

Then came the most legendary repair in space history. On Apollo 17, a hammer in Cernan's pocket caught the fender and snapped it off. Moon dust sprays everywhere without a fender, so they used duct tape. The dust ruined the tape. The next morning the crew taped four laminated maps together, clamped them on, and it held for the rest of the mission. The Apollo 17 crew drove about 35 km, and rocks brought home grew from about 21.5 kg (Apollo 11) to 111 kg (Apollo 17).

The buggies stayed because the lander could only lift so much, and every kilogram of buggy meant less Moon rock for Earth. Three Moon Buggies are still parked up there.

What the Moon Buggy teaches us: fix it with what you have. A smarter idea beats a better tool.

2. Spirit: the rover that drove backwards

Spirit landed on January 4, 2004 in Gusev Crater. The plan: 90 Mars days. It lasted about 20 times longer and drove almost 5 miles (8 km). Then one wheel stopped working. So engineers drove it backwards, dragging the dead wheel like a shopping cart with one stubborn wheel.

The twist: the dragging wheel dug a trench that uncovered bright, silica-rich soil, which scientists read as a sign of ancient hot water or steam. Broken things find things. In 2009 Spirit got stuck in soft sand, went silent in March 2010, and NASA ended the mission in May 2011.

What Spirit teaches us: a problem can become a discovery if you try a different direction, even backwards.

3. Opportunity: the marathon runner who was only supposed to walk 1 km

If Spirit was the stubborn one, Opportunity was the overachiever: asked for a one-page assignment, it handed in a 28-page thesis. Plan: 90 Mars days, about 1 km. Result: 5,111 Mars days, 45.16 km (28.06 miles), which is farther than a marathon, plus 200,000+ raw images and the record for the longest drive on another world. It found tiny "blueberries" and rocks showing the area was once soaked in water. And Mars helped: winds kept blowing its solar panels clean.

In June 2018 a planet-wide dust storm hid the Sun. Last contact: June 10, 2018. NASA sent more than 1,000 commands over eight months, then declared the mission complete on February 13, 2019. Opportunity never quit. It simply ran out of sunlight.

What Opportunity teaches us: nobody can tell you how far you can go. Run your own marathon.

4. Ingenuity: the helicopter that didn't read the rules

Ingenuity weighed 1.8 kg (4 pounds), about two bags of sugar. Mars air is about 1% as thick as Earth's, so its blades spun about 2,400 times a minute. NASA planned up to 5 flights in 30 days. On April 19, 2021 it made the first powered, controlled flight on another planet, carrying a tiny swatch of fabric from the original Wright Flyer.

  • 72 flights and 128.8 minutes in the air
  • 17 km (10.5 miles) flown, more than 14 times the plan
  • Up to 24 m high, top speed about 36 km/h
  • Almost 1,000 Mars days, more than 33 times longer than planned

On January 18, 2024, flight 72 damaged its rotor blades on landing. It still stands upright on Mars, in contact with Earth, but it will never fly again. The mission cost about $85 million, and NASA is already planning future Mars helicopters based on it.

What Ingenuity teaches us: "impossible" often just means nobody has done it yet.

5. Voyager 1: still texting (reply time: 24 hours)

Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977, visited Jupiter and Saturn, and left the Sun's bubble in 2012. On Wednesday, November 18, 2026 it becomes the first human-made object one light-day from Earth: about 25.9 billion km. A radio message takes 24 hours one way, so even "hello, are you there?" has a 48-hour round trip. It also carries a Golden Record with greetings in 55 languages, including Urdu. NASA expects its power to run out in the early 2030s.

What Voyager teaches us: you can be far from home, slow to hear back, and a little bit old, and still keep sending signals.

About this project

Astro Innovate Orbit is our entry for the NASA challenge "Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars". The machines tell their own stories so school students can meet the hardware and the science it made possible.

The 2064 crew, Zain, Nani and Hina Khan are fiction. Mission facts come from NASA mission pages (NASA Science: Voyager, nasa.gov). Photos: NASA, JPL-Caltech and partners, as credited. NASA does not endorse this project.

Voice narration and subtitles use your browser's built-in text-to-speech. Parts of the code and text were created with the help of an AI assistant (Claude).
